Red Team

Password Cracking

2026-10-09 · 9 min read

A great deal of red-team progress comes down to turning a captured hash into a password — a kerberoast ticket, a NetNTLM hash from poisoning, a SAM/NTDS dump, or a KeePass/ZIP file. Cracking is less about raw horsepower than about strategy: identifying the hash, then spending your compute where passwords actually live.

Identify the hash first

The mode decides everything — speed, approach, and whether it is worth attempting. Fast hashes (NTLM, NetNTLMv1/v2, raw MD5/SHA) fall to large wordlists and rules; deliberately slow ones (bcrypt, Argon2, and the iterated KDFs behind KeePass/LUKS) demand small, targeted guesses. 

# hashcat mode examples
# 1000 = NTLM   5600 = NetNTLMv2   13100 = Kerberoast (TGS-REP)   18200 = AS-REP
hashid ''            # or name-that-hash
hashcat -m 1000 hashes.txt rockyou.txt

The strategy, in order

  1. Wordlist first — rockyou and curated real-password lists catch the easy majority cheaply.
  2. Wordlist + rules — rule sets (best64, OneRuleToRuleThemAll) mutate words the way humans do (capitalise, append a year/symbol). This is usually the highest-yield step.
  3. Targeted wordlists — build candidates from OSINT: company name, local sports teams, product names, seasons; crack the org's own pattern, not the internet's.
  4. Masks (brute within a pattern) — when you know the shape (e.g. Word + 4 digits), a mask attack covers it exhaustively and cheaply.
  5. Combinator / hybrid — join words, or append a mask to a wordlist, for passphrase-style passwords.
  6. Pure brute force — last resort, and only viable for short passwords or fast hashes.
The highest-value move is almost always a good wordlist plus a strong rule set, seeded with target-specific words. Humans build passwords from meaningful roots and predictable mutations; crack the human, not the keyspace.

Tools

  • hashcat — GPU-accelerated, the standard for volume; supports every mode and attack above.
  • John the Ripper — flexible, great for odd formats and its *2john helpers (zip2john, keepass2john, etc.) that extract a crackable hash from a file.
  • Wordlists & rules — SecLists, weakpass, and curated rule files; plus custom lists from the target's own site and documents.

Feeding it back

Cracked passwords are intelligence: they reveal the org's password pattern, which turns a few cracks into a high-probability spraying list for everyone else, and they expose reuse across services. A single Company@2025 often unlocks far more than the one account it came from.

Defensive takeaways

  • Length beats complexity: long passphrases and a banned-common-password policy defeat wordlist+rules far better than character rules do.
  • Slow, salted hashing (Argon2/bcrypt) for stored passwords makes offline cracking uneconomical.
  • For AD, protect against the capture in the first place — strong service-account passwords / gMSA defeat kerberoasting, and signing/EPA defeat relay.
  • MFA blunts the value of a cracked password on anything that supports it.
