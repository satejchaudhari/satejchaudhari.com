Red Team

Phishing & Pretext Delivery

2026-10-15 · 10 min read

Phishing is the most common way real intrusions start, and on a red-team engagement it is usually the cheapest route past a hardened perimeter: you are not fighting the firewall, you are convincing a person to run your code or hand over a credential. This page maps how a modern phishing operation is built and, just as importantly, how each stage is detected — so the control you are testing is one you can reason about.

The anatomy of a campaign

Every operation decomposes into the same stages, and defenders hold a control at each one:

StageWhat happensPrimary control
PretextA believable story — IT notice, invoice, HR policy, shared documentUser awareness, reporting culture
InfrastructureLook-alike domain, sender, landing page, redirectorDomain age/reputation, DMARC, URL rewriting
DeliveryEmail, SMS (smishing), voice (vishing), LinkedIn, TeamsSecure email gateway, attachment detonation
Lure actionClick a link, open an attachment, enter a credential, approve MFAMFA type, browser isolation, EDR
Payload / harvestCredential capture, token theft, or code executionEDR, conditional access, logging

Pretext and target selection

The pretext is the product. It is built from OSINT (see OSINT Fundamentals): the email format, the tools a company advertises in job posts, the people whose roles make a request plausible, and the events — a merger, a migration, a benefits window — that make urgency believable. Targeting trades breadth for stealth: one well-researched spear-phish against a service desk or developer is quieter and more effective than a thousand-recipient blast that a single report burns.

Infrastructure and sender trust

Mail only lands if it survives authentication. Three records decide this, and you check a target's posture with public DNS:

dig +short TXT example.com         | grep spf      # who may send for the domain
dig +short TXT default._domainkey.example.com       # DKIM selector (signing key)
dig +short TXT _dmarc.example.com                   # policy: none | quarantine | reject
# Entra: does a username exist / is the domain federated?
curl -s "https://login.microsoftonline.com/getuserrealm.srf?login=user@example.com&xml=1"

Operators register a look-alike domain, age it, warm the sending IP, and set SPF/DKIM/DMARC correctly for their own domain — so the mail is technically authentic, just not from who it appears to be. Look-alikes exploit homoglyphs, extra words (company-support.com), or alternate TLDs. A redirector sits in front of the payload so scanners never see the real host.

Delivery beyond email

Email gateways are the most mature control, so operators move to weaker channels: smishing (SMS + short link), vishing (a call to the help desk — the 2023–24 route to resetting an MFA factor), and chat (Teams/Slack from a compromised or look-alike tenant, often unfiltered). The pretext adapts to the channel; the goal does not.

The two payload families

Everything downstream is one of two things:

  1. Credential / token harvest — a landing page that captures a password, or a proxy that captures the post-MFA session (see AiTM, Token & Session Theft).
  2. Code execution — an attachment or linked file that runs a loader for a C2 foothold (see Payload & Maldoc Tradecraft).

Which one you choose is dictated by the target's controls: phishing-resistant MFA kills credential harvest, so you pivot to execution; strict application control makes execution hard, so you pivot to token theft.

How phishing is stopped

  • Mail layer: DMARC reject on look-alikes, attachment detonation, time-of-click URL rewriting, executive-impersonation rules.
  • Identity layer: phishing-resistant MFA (FIDO2/WebAuthn) neutralises credential and token phishing because the authenticator is bound to the origin; number-matching blunts MFA fatigue.
  • Endpoint layer: EDR on the execution chain; ASR rules blocking Office child processes and macros from the internet.
  • Human layer: a fast, blame-free report button is the single highest-value control.

On an authorised engagement the deliverable is not "we phished N people" but "here is which control failed, and where" — the matrix at the top is how you frame it.
