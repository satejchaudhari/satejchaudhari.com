Red Team

Phishing & Pretext Delivery

2026-10-15 · 10 min read

Phishing is the most common way real intrusions start, and on a red-team engagement it is usually the cheapest route past a hardened perimeter: you are not fighting the firewall, you are convincing a person to run your code or hand over a credential. This page is the conceptual map of how a modern phishing operation is built and, just as importantly, how each stage is detected — so the controls you are testing are the ones you can actually reason about.

The anatomy of a campaign

Every phishing operation decomposes into the same stages, and defenders have a control at each one:

StageWhat happensPrimary control
PretextA believable story — IT notice, invoice, HR policy, shared documentUser awareness, reporting culture
InfrastructureLook-alike domain, mail sender, landing page, redirectorDomain age/reputation, DMARC, URL rewriting
DeliveryEmail, SMS (smishing), voice (vishing), LinkedIn, TeamsSecure email gateway, attachment detonation
Lure actionClick a link, open an attachment, enter a credential, approve MFAMFA type, browser isolation, EDR
Payload / harvestCredential capture, token theft, or code executionEDR, conditional access, logging

Pretext and target selection

The pretext is the product. It is built from OSINT (see OSINT Fundamentals): the organisation's email format, the tools it advertises in job posts, the people whose roles make a request plausible, and the events (a merger, a migration, a benefits-enrolment window) that make urgency believable. Targeting trades breadth for stealth — a single well-researched spear-phish against a service desk or a developer is quieter and more effective than a thousand-recipient blast that one report burns.

Infrastructure and sender trust

Mail only lands if it survives authentication. Three records decide this:

  • SPF says which servers may send for a domain.
  • DKIM cryptographically signs the message.
  • DMARC ties the two to the visible From: and tells receivers what to do on failure (none / quarantine / reject).

Operators acquire a domain, age it, warm the sending IP, and configure all three correctly for their own look-alike domain — so the mail is technically authentic, just not from who it appears to be. Look-alikes exploit homoglyphs, extra words (company-support.com), or alternate TLDs. A redirector or reverse proxy sits between the victim and the real payload so the sensitive host is never exposed to scanners.

Delivery vectors beyond email

Email gateways are the most mature control, so operators increasingly move to channels with weaker filtering: smishing (SMS with a short link), vishing (a phone call to the help desk — the classic 2023–2024 route to resetting an MFA factor), and chat (Teams/Slack messages from a compromised or look-alike tenant, which many organisations do not filter at all). The pretext adapts to the channel; the goal is unchanged.

The two payload families

Everything downstream is one of two things:

  1. Credential / token harvest — a landing page that captures a password, or an adversary-in-the-middle proxy that captures the post-MFA session (covered in depth in AiTM, Token & Session Theft).
  2. Code execution — an attachment or linked file that runs a loader, giving a C2 foothold (covered in Payload & Maldoc Tradecraft).

Which one you choose is dictated by the target's controls: if MFA is phishing-resistant (FIDO2), credential harvest is pointless and you pivot to execution; if application control is strict, execution is hard and you pivot to token theft.

How phishing is detected and stopped

  • Mail layer: DMARC reject on look-alikes, attachment detonation sandboxes, URL rewriting/time-of-click checks, and impersonation rules for executive names.
  • Identity layer: phishing-resistant MFA (FIDO2/WebAuthn) neutralises both credential and token phishing because the authenticator is bound to the origin; number-matching blunts MFA fatigue.
  • Endpoint layer: EDR on the execution chain, ASR rules blocking Office child processes and macros from the internet.
  • Human layer: a fast, blame-free report button is the single highest-value control — it turns one alert user into early warning for everyone.

On an authorised engagement the deliverable is not "we phished N people" but "here is which control failed and where" — the matrix at the top of this page is how you frame it.
