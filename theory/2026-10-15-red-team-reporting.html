Red Team Reporting & Deliverables

2026-10-15 · 9 min read

A red-team engagement is only as valuable as what the client can act on afterwards. The report is the deliverable — not the shells. Turning an operation into findings an organisation can prioritise and fix is a distinct skill from gaining access, and it is what separates a professional engagement from a joyride. This complements Red Team Methodology & Threat Emulation.

Who reads it, and what they need

AudienceWants
Executives / boardBusiness risk in plain language: what could happen, how bad, are we better than last time
Security leadershipWhich controls failed, systemic themes, prioritised remediation
Engineers / ITExact reproduction steps, affected assets, specific fixes
Blue teamTimeline and IOCs to validate their detections against your activity

A good report serves all four without making any one of them wade through the others' material — hence the structure below.

Structure of a red-team report

  1. Executive summary — the story in one page: scope, what you achieved, business impact, headline recommendations. No jargon.
  2. Engagement overview — scope, rules of engagement, timeline, the threat you emulated.
  3. Attack narrative — the kill chain told as a story: initial access → escalation → lateral movement → objective, with evidence. This is what makes a red-team report distinct from a vuln scan.
  4. Findings — each with risk rating, affected assets, reproduction, impact, and remediation.
  5. Detection & response analysis — what the blue team saw, what they missed, where detection should improve (the "purple" value).
  6. Strategic recommendations — systemic fixes, not just per-finding patches.
  7. Appendices — full IOC list, tooling, timeline for the defenders.

A finding, written well

Each finding follows the same skeleton so a reader can scan it fast:

Title:        Kerberoastable service account with a weak password
Risk:         High   (Likelihood: High — any domain user; Impact: High — DA path)
Affected:     svc_sql (SPN MSSQLSvc/sql01.corp.local)
ATT&CK:       T1558.003 (Kerberoasting)
Evidence:     TGS requested as low-priv user; cracked offline in 4 minutes
Reproduction: 1) request the SPN ticket  2) crack the RC4 hash offline
Impact:       svc_sql is local admin on 30 hosts and in Account Operators
Remediation:  30+ char managed/gMSA password; AES-only; alert on mass TGS requests

Rate findings honestly

Risk is impact × likelihood in the client's context, not raw CVSS. A medium-severity bug on the only path to the crown jewels outranks a "critical" on an isolated test box. Tie each finding to business impact and to the control that failed — "phishing-resistant MFA was not enforced", not merely "we phished a user". The audit matrix in Phishing & Pretext Delivery is an example of framing by failed control.

A simple likelihood × impact matrix keeps ratings consistent across findings rather than argued one at a time:

 Low impactHigh impact
LikelyMediumCritical
UnlikelyLowHigh

The attack narrative is the point

Scanners produce finding lists; red teams produce chains. The value of the narrative is showing how individually-minor issues combined into total compromise, so the client understands that fixing any one link breaks the chain. Keep it evidence-backed (screenshots, timestamps, hostnames) and mapped to MITRE ATT&CK so defenders can line it up against their coverage.

Remediation and retest

  • Make fixes specific and prioritised — ordered by risk, with quick wins flagged separately from strategic work.
  • Prefer systemic fixes — one tiering or MFA change often closes a whole class of findings.
  • Offer a retest so the client can prove the chain is broken, and the engagement ends on evidence, not assertion.
